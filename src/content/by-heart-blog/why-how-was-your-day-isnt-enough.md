"How was your day?" is the most common thing partners ask each other, and it's also one of the easiest questions in the world to answer without saying anything. "Fine." "Busy." "Same as always." Three words, conversation over, both people back to their phones.

It's not that the question is wrong. It's that it's unspecific, and unspecific questions get unspecific answers. Your partner's brain doesn't have to search for anything interesting to say — "fine" is always true enough to pass as an answer, so it's the path of least resistance.

## Specific beats open-ended

The fix isn't asking more often. It's asking something narrower. "How was your day" invites a summary. "What was the most annoying part of your day" or "did that meeting with your manager happen" invites a story, because it points at something real instead of everything at once.

This is really just the same thing that makes any suggestion feel good instead of generic: specificity. "Do something nice" is advice nobody can act on. "Bring back the coffee she always gets when she's stressed" is something you can actually do this afternoon. The gap between the two is attention — you have to have noticed something and remembered it — but noticing is a skill you can build, not a trait you either have or don't.

> "Fine" is always true enough to pass as an answer, so it's the path of least resistance.

## Where the material comes from

You don't need a better memory. You need somewhere to keep what you already know: the way your partner takes their coffee when work is bad, what actually calmed them down last time they were anxious, what they mentioned in passing three weeks ago that you're pretty sure they're still thinking about. Once that's written down somewhere, it stops being effort to remember and starts being a place to look.

That's most of what a good check-in question is: something specific enough that it couldn't apply to just anyone. It shows you were paying attention before you even asked.
