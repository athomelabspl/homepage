<AISummary>
- Feeling like the only one trying is a common experience and doesn't by itself mean something is wrong with you or the relationship.
- Long-term research on couples (Gottman's studies on "bids for connection") found that relationship stability depends on both partners responding to each other's small bids over time, not on one partner compensating indefinitely.
- A "bid" is any small attempt at connection: a comment, a look, a "you won't believe this." How a partner responds to it (turning toward, away, or against) is more predictive of stability than how couples handle big arguments.
- Effort and outcome are different things: showing up consistently doesn't guarantee a relationship works, and it not working out isn't proof someone tried too little.
- A long-standing, one-sided pattern (one that hasn't shifted even after being named) is worth raising directly, or with a therapist, rather than quietly working around.
</AISummary>

You keep the birthday in your head. You notice when something's off before it's said out loud. You're the one who suggests the walk, asks the follow-up question, remembers how the meeting with their boss actually went. And somewhere in the middle of doing all of that, a quieter thought shows up: *am I the only one doing this?*

That thought isn't a character flaw, and it isn't proof of anything either. **It's information.** The question worth asking isn't whether the feeling is fair (if you're having it, it almost certainly is), but what to actually do with it.

## What this tends to look like

It rarely announces itself as one big moment. More often it's a slow accumulation of small things: you're the one who remembers anniversaries, they're the one who gets reminded. You ask how their day went and mean it; the question doesn't come back the other way. You notice when they're quiet and check in; when you're quiet, it goes unremarked. None of these, on its own, means much. **Add them up over months, though, and the pattern is real,** even if no single incident would hold up as evidence to a stranger.

## What the research actually shows

Some of the most useful work on this doesn't come from couples already in crisis. Psychologist John Gottman spent years following newlywed couples, tracking something small: how they responded to each other's "bids" for attention. A bid is anything from a comment about the weather to "look at this" to a sigh loud enough to be an invitation. Gottman found three ways people respond: turning toward a bid (engaging, even briefly), turning away (missing it, distracted), or turning against it (irritated, dismissive).

Six years later, he checked back in. Couples who were still together had **turned toward each other's bids about 86% of the time**. Couples who'd since divorced had done it only about a third of the time.

The finding wasn't that one devoted partner saved the relationship by trying hard enough for two. **It was that both people, responding to each other, over years, made the difference.** That's worth sitting with for two reasons: your own consistency is genuinely doing something, and it was never going to be enough by itself.

## What effort can change, and what it can't

Your own effort can reliably do a few things. It can make you someone who notices, who follows through, who's genuinely pleasant to be around. It can shift your own half of the pattern, which sometimes (not always) invites a response. It can tell you, clearly, what you're capable of and willing to give. **What it can't do is manufacture the other half.** It can't make someone respond who consistently doesn't. It can't turn a pattern that's been stable for years into a different one through sheer persistence.

> Effort and outcome are different things. Showing up well doesn't guarantee a relationship works, and it not working out isn't proof you didn't try hard enough.

## Telling a rough patch from a real pattern

Not every stretch like this means something lasting. A hard season (a new baby, a brutal work quarter, a death in the family) can make anyone turn inward for a while, and that's not the same as a settled dynamic. A few things are worth checking without flinching: has this been going on for a specific, nameable reason, or has it just always been this way? Has it changed at all after you've actually said something out loud, or does it snap back within a week? And is there any reciprocity at all, even if it's not equal, or does the noticing only ever run one direction?

None of these questions has a clean pass or fail answer. **But sitting with them plainly usually tells you more than the feeling alone does.**

## A practice worth keeping, either way

None of this is a reason to stop. **Becoming someone who notices, who follows through, who remembers the small things is a good way to be**, and it doesn't stop being worth it just because the relationship's outcome is out of your hands. Think of it less as a strategy for fixing the relationship and more as a habit you're building in yourself, one that happens to also be good for the relationship when it lands. That's the small piece By Heart is built to help with: not fixing anything on your behalf, just making the noticing itself a little easier to keep up with, one nudge at a time.

It's also fine to notice when it isn't landing. If this has gone on a long time, if it's rarely reciprocated, if you're tired in a way that isn't lifting, that's not something to push past on willpower. It might be worth saying out loud to your partner, plainly, without keeping score. And if you're far enough into it that you're not sure what to do next, **a therapist is a much better resource for that question than any article**, including this one.

Showing up for someone is worth doing well. It was never supposed to be something you do alone forever.
