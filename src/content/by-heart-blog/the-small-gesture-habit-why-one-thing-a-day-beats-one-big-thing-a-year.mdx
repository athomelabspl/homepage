Ask most people what makes a relationship work, and they'll describe a moment: a proposal, an anniversary trip, a grand apology after a big fight. Ask a researcher who's spent years tracking couples, and **the answer gets much smaller: whether someone looked up from their phone when their partner said "hey, look at this."**

## What Gottman's research found

Psychologist John Gottman and his colleague Robert Levenson spent decades running what's sometimes called the "Love Lab," bringing couples in to talk, argue, and just exist together while researchers observed. One of the things they tracked was small: how often each partner responded to the other's "bids," brief, easy-to-miss attempts at connection. A comment about the weather. A sigh. "Look at this."

**Couples who stayed together over the years responded to each other's bids far more consistently than couples who split up.** The pattern held up better than how couples handled arguments or big life events. It wasn't about avoiding conflict. It was about what happened in between the conflicts, in the hundreds of small, forgettable moments that make up most of a week.

## What "small" really looks like

A bid can be almost nothing. Your partner mentions a strange dream they had. They make a small joke while cooking. They go quiet after a hard call and glance over. None of these are requests, exactly. **They're closer to an opening: a chance to turn toward someone, or to miss them entirely.**

Turning toward doesn't require much. A "wait, tell me more" instead of a distracted "mm-hm." Putting the phone down for the ten seconds the joke needs. Asking what the quiet is about instead of letting it pass. None of it is dramatic. That's the point: it doesn't need to be.

## Why consistency beats scale

There's a comforting idea that a big enough gesture can make up for a long stretch of not paying attention, an elaborate anniversary, an expensive gift, a grand apology. Sometimes it helps in the moment. **It doesn't reliably undo a pattern.** Gottman's conflict research found something related: during a disagreement, stable couples had roughly five positive interactions for every negative one. Not zero negative, five to one. The ratio wasn't built out of avoiding hard moments; it was built out of a surplus of small good ones surrounding them, the ordinary week's worth of noticing that happens whether or not there's a fight to balance out.

> A single grand gesture is one data point, easy to remember and easy to overweight. A pattern of small ones, most of them forgotten individually by the time a week has passed, is the real signal underneath it.

## What a daily practice can look like

None of this requires a personality change. It requires noticing, most days, one real thing. In practice, that tends to fall into a few repeatable shapes:

- Following up on something they mentioned in passing, a day or two later
- Asking a specific question instead of a general one ("how did the call with your sister go" instead of "how was your day")
- Putting the phone down for the length of a story, not just the first sentence of it
- Doing one small thing you already know they'd appreciate, without waiting to be asked

None of these take long. What they have in common is that they require noticing something first, then following through on it. That's the entire habit By Heart is built around: not one big yearly effort, but the daily practice of noticing something small and doing something about it.

Some days that'll be easy. Some days you'll miss it entirely, and that's fine too. The research isn't about perfection. It's about a pattern that tilts toward paying attention more often than not, and **over months, that tilt is what adds up.**
