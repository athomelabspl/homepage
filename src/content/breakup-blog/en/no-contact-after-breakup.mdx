If you are reading this, you are probably trying to do one of two things: get through this intact, or get your ex back. Most likely some mix of both.

Both are honest reasons to be here.

This article focuses on the first one. But it will not pretend the second one does not exist. Wanting them back is completely understandable, and if you are honest about that, you will make better decisions.

## What No Contact Is Not

**A manipulation tactic.** The "make them miss you" framing has been around for years. There is no reliable research showing it works as a strategy to bring someone back. More importantly, if your recovery depends on what your ex does next, you have handed the controls to someone else. That is a difficult position to be in regardless of outcome.

**A punishment or moral stance.** You are not making a statement and you do not need to announce it. Choosing not to be in contact is a decision about your own state, not a verdict on theirs.

**A streak you lose.** If you send one message and feel worse afterwards, that is not a failure that resets a counter. No contact is a direction, not a score.

## What It Actually Does

Every time you see their name, read an old message, or check their profile, your brain treats that as another encounter with someone it has been attached to. The attachment response activates. Your body does not distinguish between the relationship being alive and you simply looking at a photo.

Research by Fisher and colleagues (2010) found that, after romantic rejection, activity continues in the brain's reward and motivation systems. The response looks less like sadness and more like craving. Distance, in that context, is a physiological requirement as much as an emotional one.

**On co-regulation and withdrawal.** Sbarra and Hazan (2008) describe how, in a long relationship, partners regulate each other's emotional states. Your system learns to use the other person as a source of calm. When that contact stops abruptly, the symptoms can look like withdrawal: poor sleep, agitation, a persistent low-grade alertness. Those are expected responses when a regulatory relationship ends suddenly, not signs you are handling this badly.

Reducing contact gives your system a chance to stop waiting. Removing the trigger is what lets the loop settle.

**On autonomy and recovery.** Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 2000) holds that people recover better when their actions feel chosen rather than reactive. Staying in loose contact with an ex tends to put you in a reactive position: you check the phone, they respond or they do not, your mood shifts to match. No contact changes that. Your day is no longer structured around their behaviour.

## What It Does Not Do

**Guarantee closure.** Closure is mostly something you work through yourself. Distance does not produce it automatically, but it does reduce the noise enough for that process to start.

**Make your ex come back reliably.** Some people do reach out after a period of no contact. Most do not, or do so for reasons that have nothing to do with distance. Building your recovery around that outcome keeps you dependent on their decisions.

**Work if you replace it with proxy contact.** Checking their social media, asking mutual friends how they are doing, looking at old photographs: these have much the same effect on your attachment system as direct contact. The trigger is the exposure, not the channel. Checking their Instagram twice a day while technically not messaging them is not giving your system the break you think it is.

## How to Actually Start

**Mute, unfollow, or block.** Choose the level that works for you. There is no hierarchy. Blocking is not dramatic. It is a setting, and it is there to reduce your own access, not to send a message to them. If you know you will unmute within a few days, use the stronger option.

**Tell one person you trust.** Not for accountability in any punitive sense. Having someone who knows what you are trying to do makes it easier to call them in the moments you want to reach out, rather than reaching out to your ex.

**Plan for the urge, not the rule.** The urge to reach out tends to hit hard and then pass within about 20 minutes. Have something specific ready for those windows before they arrive: a walk, a call, a task with a clear end point. You do not need to resist the feeling for weeks on end. You need to get through the next short stretch.

**If you slip, note it and keep going.** One message does not mean you failed. It means you were in a high-craving moment and the urge won that round. What is useful is noticing what triggered it: the time of day, a song, a date that means something. That information helps you prepare better next time, not feel worse now.

## You Do Not Have to Do This Perfectly

No contact is not about willpower. It is about giving your system enough space from a high-activation trigger that it can come down from a state of alert.

You do not have to be perfect at it. The benefit comes from the general reduction in exposure, not from maintaining an unbroken count.

The discomfort you feel when you stop the contact is real, and it tells you something. It tells you how much your nervous system had come to rely on this person for regulation. That is a measure of how present the relationship was, not a measure of how long recovery will take.
