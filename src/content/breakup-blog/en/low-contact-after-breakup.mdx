- Low contact means cutting off emotional access while keeping functional access.
- Treat communication with your ex like a transaction with a distant colleague.
- Keep messages brief, neutral, and strictly about the required topic.
- Expect a physical anxiety spike when you see their name, and have a plan to settle before you reply.

People always tell you to just block your ex and move on.

That advice is useless when you share a child, a dog, or a desk at work. You feel left out of the standard breakup recovery conversation. You cannot simply disappear. You still have to interact with the person who broke your heart.

Seeing their name on your screen is exhausting. But you can protect your peace without walking away from your responsibilities. You just need to switch to low contact.

## Redefining the Goal

Low contact is different from no contact.

With no contact, the goal is silence. With low contact, the goal is boundary management. You eliminate emotional access while maintaining functional access.

You are no longer their confidant. You do not ask how their day went. You do not comfort them when they are stressed. You only discuss the kids, the house, or the project at hand. The romantic relationship is over. A practical relationship has to take its place.

## The Business Protocol

To survive low contact, treat your ex like a distant coworker. You need clear rules for how and when you communicate.

### Keep It Brief and Neutral

Only share facts. Do not add emotional commentary. If you are arranging a custody drop-off, state the time and location. You do not need a long story about why you are five minutes late. "Running five minutes late" is enough.

### Change the Channel

Texting feels intimate. If a text ruins your morning, move conversations to email. Email creates mental distance. It feels formal. It also gives you time to pause before you hit send. If you are co-parenting, use a dedicated family calendar app instead of texting.

### Do Not Take the Bait

Your ex might try to start an emotional argument. They might bring up the past. They might send a nostalgic text late at night. You do not have to respond to anything outside your functional duties. Silence is a valid response to an emotional trap.

## Managing the Physical Aftermath

Even a simple email about a work project can make your heart race. Your hands might shake. Your chest gets tight. That reaction is normal. Your body is treating the contact as a threat.

The goal is not to prevent the spike. The goal is to manage it so you do not reply from anger or panic. Come back to baseline before you type.

When their name pops up and adrenaline spikes, you need an immediate anchor. A few rounds of 4-1-5 breathing, inhale for four, hold for one, out for five, can take the edge off in real time. Then send the short, boundaried reply.
